#סיפור Inside a Cloud Outage | קשה
On a quiet morning, thousands of websites around the world stopped working at the same time. Online stores could not accept payments, smart home devices stopped responding, and even the status page of the cloud provider, the page that should inform customers about problems, was unavailable. The outage lasted almost five hours.

The postmortem that followed revealed that the root cause was surprisingly small. An engineer was doing routine maintenance on a storage system. He intended to remove a small number of servers, but a typo in a command removed a much larger set. Two critical parts of the system lost the capacity they needed and had to be restarted completely. Because the system had grown enormously over the years, the restart took far longer than anyone had anticipated.

What turned a local mistake into a global incident was dependency. Hundreds of other services relied, directly or indirectly, on the storage system. When it failed, they failed too, in a chain reaction that engineers call a cascading failure. Ironically, the status page itself was hosted on the same infrastructure, so the provider could not even tell its customers what was happening.

The provider's response focused on systemic changes rather than on blaming the engineer. The command was modified so that it removes capacity more slowly and refuses to go below a safe minimum. The restart process was designed again to be significantly faster. And the status page was moved to independent infrastructure.

For the whole industry, the incident highlighted an uncomfortable truth. Cloud computing reduces many risks, but it concentrates others. When thousands of organizations depend on a single provider, a single mistake can affect them all simultaneously. Resilient architectures therefore require not only redundancy inside one provider, but also a clear understanding of which dependencies could fail together.

#סיפור Redundancy Is Not the Same as Resilience | קשה
When engineers want a system to survive failures, their first instinct is redundancy: two servers instead of one, two power supplies, two network connections. If one component fails, the other takes over. This principle is fundamental, and it prevents countless outages. Yet experience shows that redundancy alone does not guarantee resilience, and in some cases it can even create a false sense of security.

The first problem is shared dependencies. Two servers in the same data center may both fail if the building loses power. Two databases may both become corrupted if they receive the same faulty data. Two copies of the same software will both crash when they encounter the same bug. Redundancy protects against independent failures, but many real failures are correlated: one cause affects all the copies simultaneously.

The second problem is that backup components are often not tested. A secondary server may remain idle for years, and nobody notices that its configuration is outdated until the day it is needed. Ironically, the switch from the primary to the secondary component is itself a complex operation, and it is a common source of new failures.

The third problem is complexity. Every redundant component adds code, configuration and monitoring. A system with many layers of redundancy can become so complex that engineers no longer understand how it behaves when there is pressure.

Resilience, therefore, is a bigger concept. A resilient system is designed to continue functioning, perhaps with reduced capacity, even when parts of it fail in unexpected ways. It includes redundancy, but also diversity, regular testing of failover procedures, graceful degradation, and, most importantly, people who understand the system and can respond when the unexpected happens.

#סיפור The Ethics of Recognizing Faces | קשה
Facial recognition technology can identify a person from a photograph or a video in a fraction of a second. Its applications are already widespread: phones unlock when they see their owner, airports verify passengers without checking passports manually, and police in some countries use cameras to search for suspects in crowds. Supporters argue that the technology makes life more convenient and society safer. Critics, however, raise serious ethical concerns.

The first concern is accuracy. Several studies have found that some facial recognition systems make significantly more errors with women and with people with darker skin. The reason is usually the training data: if a system learns mainly from photographs of one group, it makes more mistakes with others. When such a system is used by police, an error is not merely a technical problem. It can lead to the arrest of an innocent person, and there have already been documented cases of exactly this.

The second concern is privacy. A person can change a password, but not a face. When cameras in public spaces are connected to recognition systems, it becomes possible to track the movements of citizens continuously, often without their knowledge or consent. Critics warn that such surveillance can discourage people from participating in protests or visiting sensitive places, even if they have done nothing wrong.

Responses to these concerns vary widely. Some cities have banned the use of facial recognition by public agencies. Other governments have introduced regulations that require transparency, independent audits, and human review of every match. Some technology companies have voluntarily stopped selling the technology to police.

The debate illustrates a bigger principle: the question is not only whether a technology works, but who uses it, for what purpose, and with which constraints.

#סיפור Never Trust, Always Verify | קשה
For many years, organizations protected their networks like medieval castles. A strong wall, the firewall, surrounded the internal network, and everything inside the wall was considered trustworthy. Once an employee or a device was inside, it could access most internal systems with few additional checks.

This model has become increasingly obsolete. Employees now work from home, from restaurants and from airports. Applications run in the cloud rather than in the office. And attackers have repeatedly proved that once they breach the wall, often through a single phishing email, they can move freely inside the network for weeks before anyone notices.

The alternative approach is called zero trust. Its main principle is simple: no user and no device is trusted automatically, regardless of its location. Every request to access a resource must be verified explicitly. The system checks the identity of the user, often with authentication in two steps, the condition of the device, such as whether it is updated and encrypted, and the context of the request, such as the time and the location. Moreover, each user receives only the minimum privileges necessary for his work, so that a compromised account cannot reach everything.

Implementing zero trust is not a single product that a company can buy. It is an architecture that requires careful planning, and it can take years. Legacy systems that were designed for the old model must be adapted or replaced. Employees may be frustrated by additional verification steps, so the design must balance security with convenience.

Nevertheless, many security experts consider zero trust the most realistic response to modern threats. The assumption is no longer that attackers can be kept outside. The assumption is that they may already be inside, and the architecture must limit the damage that they can cause.

#סיפור Will Quantum Computers Break Encryption? | קשה
Almost every secure connection on the internet, from online banking to private messages, relies on public-key encryption. Its security is based on mathematical problems that ordinary computers cannot solve in a reasonable time. For example, multiplying two very large prime numbers is easy, but finding those two numbers from their product would take a classical computer longer than the age of the universe.

Quantum computers work in a fundamentally different way. Instead of bits that are 0 or 1, they use qubits, which can represent combinations of states at the same time. For certain specific problems, this property allows a quantum computer to explore many possibilities in parallel. In 1994, a mathematician described an algorithm that would allow a sufficiently powerful quantum computer to solve exactly the problems on which public-key encryption depends.

Today's quantum computers are still far too small and too unreliable to threaten real encryption. They contain relatively few qubits, and those qubits are extremely sensitive to noise. Experts disagree about when, or even whether, a quantum computer powerful enough to break current encryption will exist. Estimates range from one decade to several decades.

Nevertheless, security agencies are already taking the threat seriously, for a subtle reason. An attacker can record encrypted data today and store it until a quantum computer becomes available. Information that must remain secret for many years, such as medical records or government secrets, is therefore already at risk. This strategy is sometimes called "harvest now, decrypt later."

In response, researchers have developed new "post-quantum" algorithms based on mathematical problems that are believed to be hard even for quantum computers. Standards organizations have recently approved several of them, and large technology companies have begun to integrate them into their products. The migration will be long and complex, but the goal is to complete it before the threat becomes real.

#סיפור Knowing That Something Is Wrong Is Not Enough | קשה
Traditional monitoring answers a simple question: is the system healthy? Engineers define a set of metrics before any problem occurs, such as the use of the processor, the amount of free memory, and the number of errors in each minute. When a metric crosses a threshold, an alert is sent. This approach works well for problems that engineers can anticipate.

Modern systems, however, are built from hundreds of small services that communicate with each other across many servers. In such an environment, many failures are new and unexpected. An alert may tell engineers that the checkout page is slow, but not why. Is the cause a slow database, a network problem between two services, a recent deployment, or a single customer with a request that triggers a rare bug? Traditional dashboards often cannot answer such questions, because nobody created a metric for a problem that nobody imagined.

This is the motivation for observability. An observable system produces detailed data about its internal behavior, typically in three forms: logs, which record specific events; metrics, which measure numbers over time; and traces, which follow a single request as it travels through all the services. With this data, engineers can ask new questions after a problem occurs, without changing the code. They can, for instance, filter all the slow requests and discover that every one of them passed through the same server.

Observability is not free. Collecting and storing detailed data from thousands of services is expensive, and too much data can overwhelm the people who must interpret it. Teams must therefore decide carefully what to record and for how long.

The distinction can be summarized in one sentence: monitoring tells you that something is wrong; observability helps you understand why.

#סיפור When the Update Itself Is the Attack | קשה
Security experts usually advise users and organizations to install updates as soon as possible. Updates fix vulnerabilities, and delaying them increases risk. But what happens when the update itself is malicious?

This is the idea behind a supply chain attack. Instead of attacking thousands of targets one by one, the attacker compromises a single trusted supplier, typically a company that produces software used by many organizations. The attacker inserts malicious code into a legitimate product, and the supplier then distributes it to all its customers through its normal, signed update process. Because the update comes from a trusted source and carries a valid digital signature, security tools rarely flag it.

In one widely reported incident, attackers penetrated the build system of a company that produced network management software. For months, they quietly added a small backdoor to the official updates. Approximately 18,000 organizations, including government agencies and large corporations, installed the infected version. The backdoor remained dormant for about two weeks after installation, which made it harder to connect the malicious activity to the update. The breach was discovered only when a security company noticed suspicious activity in its own network and investigated thoroughly.

Supply chain attacks are particularly difficult to defend against, because they exploit trust, which is essential to the entire software ecosystem. An organization cannot inspect every line of code in every product that it uses. However, several measures can reduce the risk. Suppliers can protect their build systems as carefully as their products, and verify that the code they ship matches the code their developers wrote. Customers can limit the privileges of software from external suppliers, monitor its behavior after updates, and segment their networks so that one compromised product cannot reach everything.

The paradox is uncomfortable: the same update mechanism that protects us from attacks can also become the most efficient way to deliver one.

#סיפור When One Database Is Not Enough | קשה
Most applications begin with a single database. It is simple to operate, and every query sees the same, up-to-date data. But as an application grows, a single database eventually becomes a bottleneck. It can only process a limited number of queries each second, and its disk can only store a limited amount of data. Engineers then face one of the most difficult decisions in system architecture: how to distribute data across several machines.

The first common technique is replication. The system keeps one primary database, which receives all the changes, and several replicas, which receive copies of those changes. Read queries, which are usually the majority, can be directed to the replicas, so the load on the primary database decreases significantly. However, replication introduces a subtle problem. Copying changes takes time, even if only milliseconds. During that interval, a replica may return outdated data. A user who changes his profile and immediately opens the page again may, for a second, see the old version.

The second technique is sharding: dividing the data itself between several databases. For instance, users with names that begin with A to M are stored in one database, and the rest in another. Each database handles only part of the load. Sharding enables enormous scale, but it makes many operations more complex. A query that needs data from several shards must contact all of them, and keeping data consistent across shards is notoriously difficult.

These problems are not merely technical details. They show a fundamental tradeoff in distributed systems. When the network between machines fails, a system must choose between consistency, meaning that every user sees the same data, and availability, meaning that the system continues to respond. It cannot guarantee both completely. Good architects do not ignore this tradeoff; they decide explicitly which property matters more for each part of their application.

#סיפור Why High Scores Can Mislead | קשה
Every few months, a new artificial intelligence model is announced, together with impressive numbers. It scores 90 percent on a reading benchmark, solves most problems in a mathematical test, or does better than previous models on a set of programming problems. Such benchmarks are essential: without a shared way to measure performance, it would be impossible to compare models or to track progress. Nevertheless, researchers increasingly warn that benchmark results can be misleading.

The first problem is contamination. Large language models are trained on enormous amounts of text from the internet, and many benchmarks are also published on the internet. If the questions and answers of a test appeared in the training data, a high score may show that the model memorized the answers rather than real ability. Detecting such contamination is difficult, because developers often do not disclose exactly what their training data contains.

The second problem is that a benchmark measures only a narrow skill. A model that answers multiple-choice questions accurately may still fail when the same knowledge is required in a realistic conversation, where questions are ambiguous and context matters. As a well-known principle states, when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Once developers optimize their models for one specific benchmark, a higher score may not lead to changes that users actually notice.

The third problem is saturation. When the best models approach perfect scores, the benchmark can no longer distinguish between them, and researchers must design new, harder tests.

Researchers have proposed several responses: keeping some test questions secret, regularly creating new benchmarks, and evaluating models on realistic problems with human experts. No solution is perfect. The general lesson, however, is clear. A benchmark score is one part of the evidence, not a final decision. It should be interpreted with the same critical attention as any other claim.

#סיפור The Paradox of Automation | קשה
Modern planes can fly almost entirely without a pilot. Autopilot systems handle the long hours of a flight more precisely than any human, and they have contributed to a dramatic decline in accidents. Yet investigators of several serious accidents have identified a troubling pattern: when the automation suddenly fails, the pilots sometimes cannot take over effectively.

Researchers call this the paradox of automation. The more reliable an automated system becomes, the less often humans need to intervene. As a result, they practice their manual skills less, and they pay less attention to the situation. When a rare failure finally occurs, the system gives the job back to a person who is out of practice and who does not completely understand what is happening, often at the most critical second. In other words, automation removes the easy work and leaves humans with only the hardest problems.

The same phenomenon appears far beyond aviation. Drivers of partially autonomous cars may stop watching the road. Engineers who rely on automated deployment tools may forget how to recover a system manually. Even writers who depend on automatic spelling tools may lose confidence in their own spelling.

Experts propose several strategies to mitigate the problem. Pilots are required to fly manually regularly and to train in simulators for rare emergencies. People who design automated systems try to keep humans informed, for example by explaining what the system is doing and why, so that the transition is less abrupt. Some engineering teams practice failures on purpose, turning off automated tools in planned exercises.

The lesson is not that automation is dangerous. In fact, it has saved countless lives. But automation changes the role of the human from operator to supervisor, and supervising well requires skills, attention, and training of its own.

#סיפור Releasing a Feature Without Turning It On | בינוני
Many large companies release new code every day, but users do not see every new feature immediately. The reason is a technique called feature flags. A feature flag is a simple condition in the code: if the flag is on, the new feature appears; if it is off, users see the old behavior. The code of the feature is already deployed, but it remains hidden until someone switches the flag.

Separating the deployment of code from the release of a feature has significant advantages. A team can turn on a feature for internal employees first, then for one percent of users, and gradually for everyone. If a problem emerges, there is no need for an emergency rollback; an engineer simply turns the flag off.

However, feature flags have a cost. Every flag adds a branch to the code, and old flags that nobody removes slowly become confusing technical debt. In one famous incident, a large company lost hundreds of millions of dollars in less than an hour, partly because an old flag that nobody used was accidentally used again and turned on outdated code. Experienced teams therefore treat flags as temporary and delete them once a feature is completely released.

#סיפור Saying No to Protect Everyone | בינוני
Imagine a popular service that suddenly receives ten times its normal traffic, perhaps because of a news event or a bug in a client application that sends the same request again and again. If the servers try to handle every request, they may receive more than they can process, slow down for everyone, and eventually crash. Ironically, trying to serve all users can result in serving nobody.

Rate limiting prevents this situation. The service defines a maximum number of requests that each client may send in a given period, for instance 100 requests each minute. When a client goes beyond this threshold, the service rejects additional requests with a clear error message, often asking the client to retry later. In this way, one faulty client cannot consume the resources that other users need.

A related technique, load shedding, operates on the whole system. When the system approaches its capacity, it rejects the least important requests on purpose, such as background work, so that critical functions, such as payments, continue to work.

Both techniques show the same principle: a system that sometimes says "no" is more reliable than a system that tries to say "yes" to everyone and then stops working completely.

#סיפור The Canary in the Server | בינוני
In the past, miners carried small birds into coal mines. If dangerous gas was in the air, the bird would react before the miners, and they could escape in time. Software engineers borrowed this idea for a deployment strategy called a canary release.

Instead of deploying a new version to all servers simultaneously, the team first deploys it to a small fraction, for instance two percent of the traffic. Automated systems then compare the metrics of the canary servers with those of the servers that still run the old version: the number of errors, latency, and business metrics such as completed purchases. If the canary works as well as the old version, the deployment gradually expands. If the metrics become worse, the system makes a rollback of the canary automatically, and only a small share of users was affected.

Canary releases do not remove every bug, but they limit their impact. They also show an important shift in thinking: instead of assuming that testing before release can catch every problem, teams accept that some problems will appear only with real users, and they design the release process to detect them early.

#סיפור Why Pressing "Pay" Twice Should Not Charge You Twice | בינוני
Networks are unreliable. A request can arrive at a server, be processed successfully, and then the response can be lost on its way back to the client. From the point of view of the client, the request simply timed out. The natural reaction is to retry. But if the request was a payment, a simple retry can charge the customer twice.

Engineers solve this problem by designing operations to be idempotent. An idempotent operation produces the same result whether it runs once or several times. Setting the status of an order to "paid" is naturally idempotent; adding 100 dollars to an account is not. To make a payment idempotent, the client adds a unique identifier to each request. When the server receives a request, it first checks whether it has already processed that identifier. If it has, it simply returns the previous result instead of charging again.

This simple pattern is fundamental in distributed systems. It allows clients to retry safely, which in turn makes the whole system more resilient. Without it, every network failure would force engineers to choose between losing requests and processing them twice.

#סיפור The Right to Be Forgotten | בינוני
In several countries, people now have a right, by law, to ask companies to delete their personal data. In some countries, for instance, regulation allows citizens to request the deletion of data that is no longer necessary for the purpose for which it was collected. For users, this is an important protection. For engineers, it is a surprisingly difficult technical challenge.

The difficulty is that personal data rarely exists in one place. A single customer may appear in the main database, in several replicas, in backups from the last months, in log files, in systems that analyze data, and in the systems of external suppliers. Deleting a row in the main database is easy; finding and deleting every copy is not. Backups are a particular problem, because they are designed so that nobody can change them.

Organizations address this challenge in various ways. Some encrypt the data of each user with a separate key and simply destroy the key when the user asks to be forgotten, so that nobody can read any copy. Others limit retention periods, so that old backups and logs are deleted automatically. In both cases, the lesson is the same: privacy must be considered in the architecture from the beginning, not added after that.

#סיפור Paying Hackers to Find Bugs | בינוני
For many years, companies treated external security researchers who found vulnerabilities as suspects, and sometimes even threatened them with the law. Today, many large organizations take the opposite approach: they pay them. In a bug bounty program, a company publicly asks researchers to search for vulnerabilities in its products and offers a reward for each valid report, from a few hundred to hundreds of thousands of dollars, depending on how serious the problem is.

The reason is about money and risk. Attackers are already searching for vulnerabilities; the question is who finds them first. A bounty program gives ethical hackers a legitimate reason to report problems instead of selling them to criminals. It also gives the company access to thousands of experts from many backgrounds, and their skills together go beyond those of any internal team.

Such programs require careful management. Companies must define clearly which systems researchers may test, respond quickly to reports, and fix the vulnerabilities before they become public. A program that ignores reports or pays late quickly loses the trust of researchers. When managed well, however, bug bounty programs have become one of the most efficient tools in modern security.

#סיפור The End of an Old Interface | בינוני
When a company changes its API, it cannot simply delete the old version. Thousands of external applications may depend on it, and removing it in one day would break them all. For this reason, companies follow a careful process. First, they announce that the old version is deprecated: it still works, but it will be removed on a specific date, typically months or even years later. Then they publish documentation that explains how to migrate to the new version, and they monitor which clients still use the old one.

In practice, this process is rarely easy. Some developers ignore the messages, and some applications were written by people who left their companies long ago. As the deadline approaches, a significant number of clients often still depend on the old version. The company must then choose between breaking those applications and extending the deadline, which delays the benefits of the new design.

Many companies therefore make the old version gradually less convenient. They send warnings in the responses, slow down old requests a little, or temporarily turn off the old version for a few minutes to reveal hidden dependencies. These techniques are uncomfortable, but they often succeed where simple messages fail.

#סיפור When Waiting Too Long Is the Problem | בינוני
Every time one service calls another, it must decide how long to wait for an answer. This limit is called a timeout. Many developers choose a long timeout, such as one minute, because they want to give the other service every chance to respond. Surprisingly, this decision can make a system much less resilient.

Consider an online store in which the checkout service calls a service that recommends products. One day, this second service becomes extremely slow. Because the timeout is one minute, every checkout request now waits a full minute before it gives up. Meanwhile, new customers keep arriving. Within a few minutes, all the threads of the checkout service are busy with requests that are waiting, and the service cannot accept new requests at all. A problem in a small feature has stopped the most critical function of the business.

A shorter timeout, combined with a fallback, would have prevented this. If the recommended products do not arrive within half a second, the checkout page simply appears without them. Customers barely notice, and payments continue. Engineers summarize the lesson with a simple rule: never let a dependency that is not essential take down an essential one.

#שאלה Why did the checkout service stop accepting new requests? | All its threads were busy with requests that were waiting | The second service was deleted | Customers stopped arriving | The database was full | כל התהליכונים היו תפוסים בהמתנה ארוכה.
